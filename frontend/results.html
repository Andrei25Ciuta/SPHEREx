What the detector did on real images

On 7 November 2026 the detector of this app was run on real SPHEREx cutouts ( MB) of sky patches. This page shows what came out, the failures next to the successes.

No SPHEREx image or pixel is published on this page. The images stay on the author's computer; only numbers measured from them are shown. Every number is copied from docs/VALIDATION.md, which gives the command that produced it. The charts on the rest of the site (blink, difference, spectrum) run on synthetic data unless the backend is connected.

1. A blind search for fast movers

The detector (Find movers) was not told what to look for. Afterwards its candidates were compared with SIMBAD. Each field has exactly one catalogued fast mover: Barnard's Star (10.4″ a year) and Epsilon Indi B (4.7″ a year). The chart shows how far the measured motion is from the catalogue motion.

Both objects were found in every run, and nothing else was reported (no candidate without a catalogue match). The differences are 0.03–0.13 pixel per year. Searching both detectors together gave the smallest difference for each star ( and ″/yr). The two D6 differences got a little worse than in an earlier version of the code; that is in VALIDATION.md too.

2. How many of the lines are chance?

A candidate is a line through dots in several images. In a crowded field some lines happen by chance, so the detector estimates how many lines this good the dots of that field would make by chance, and keeps a candidate only if that number is below 1. Epsilon Indi B in D6 shows why: with the first version of the rule it listed the real star and three false lines.

The real lines have at most chance lines expected; the three false ones about each. The false ones are also supported by dots in only 2–3 of the images where the position could be measured, the real ones in all of them.

Not an independent test. The rule was tightened after this failure was seen on these images. A new field would be needed to test it.

3. A bright star and the bad-pixel mask

Barnard's Star is so bright that the core of its image is flagged as saturated in every exposure. The centre of a partly flagged core moves from date to date, which looks like a wrong proper motion. Fitting the star only to the unflagged pixels fixed it.

A fix, not a test. It was designed after seeing the large error on the same images. What argues against tuning: it does nothing for an unsaturated star, and synthetic tests cover it.

4. Are the brightnesses on the right scale?

For isolated, unsaturated stars the measured flux (a small aperture, in Jansky) was divided by the flux expected from the star's 2MASS or AllWISE magnitude at the wavelength of that image.

The scale is right to within about 10 %. A ratio a little below 1 is expected because the small aperture catches only part of a star's light. D6 sits lower than D2 in both fields ( against , and against ). This is not an absolute calibration, and the catalogue zero points were written from memory in the script.

5. An asteroid that moved between two images

The main-belt asteroid Arosa (V = 14.6) lies in two images of the same survey pass, hours apart. The ephemeris service SkyBoT says where it is at each time. The chart shows the flux at those two positions in each image.

The source is at the predicted position in each image and absent from the other: it moved ″ in hours (″ an hour; SkyBoT says ″). Its flux is the same at the two wavelengths, as expected of a sunlit rock, and its centre is ″ from the predicted position. It is not the brightest source of the field, which is why the proof of motion matters.

6. WISE 0855, the namesake object

WISE 0855 is the coldest known brown dwarf and far too faint to show as a dot in one exposure, so a blind search cannot find it. The weaker question: is there flux at the position its catalogue motion predicts, when all the images are added up? The same stack was made at 16 control places (the same path shifted 40 and 60 arcseconds), where nothing is expected.

Suggestive, not a detection. The stack is mJy, which is times the robust scatter of the controls, but it rests on images (two flagged ones were left out and would push in opposite directions), and one control sits on a field star (+4.81 mJy, off the chart), which makes the plain scatter of the controls so large that the target would be only 0.1 σ. The significance depends on how the controls are chosen.

D2 is not WISE 0855. At 1 µm this object is thought to be millions of times fainter than what was measured (a magnitude quoted from memory, to be checked). The flux belongs to . The app now warns when a second source sits within the measuring aperture.

7. Planet X mode on a real dwarf planet

Planet X mode looks for a body so far away that it swings across the sky as the Earth goes round the Sun, and reads its distance from that swing. Makemake (a dwarf planet at 52 AU) is in real images. Each cutout was centred on the position the ephemeris gives, and the ephemeris was otherwise used only for grading.

When told where to look, the fit works: AU against AU, with the residuals at most ″ (one pixel). It tests the Earth's position, the parallax formula and the geometry. But only degrees of freedom check the fit, and the dots were chosen with the ephemeris.

The blind search failed. Run with no ephemeris on all dots of the cutouts, it returned candidates at 52–54 AU, and of them is Makemake. The cutouts are centred on the body, so any dot near a centre follows its parallax track, and the body was detected in only of images, which lets chance combinations fit as well. A fair test needs cutouts not centred on the body and more detections. The blind Planet X search has not been shown to work on real data.
